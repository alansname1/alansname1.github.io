What Is a Good Typing Speed?

A good typing speed is not just a number. Learn how to interpret your WPM score, why accuracy and test length matter, and how to choose a realistic next goal based on your current performance.

If you just finished a typing test, you may be wondering whether your score is good. The most useful answer depends on more than WPM alone.

A typing score becomes more meaningful when you consider your accuracy, consistency, test length, experience, and reason for typing.

Someone learning the keyboard may be making excellent progress at 30 WPM. Someone who types throughout the workday may want to develop a higher comfortable speed. A person who can briefly reach 80 WPM but makes frequent errors may benefit more from accuracy practice than from trying to type even faster.

Quick answer: A useful typing speed is one that lets you complete your normal computer tasks comfortably while maintaining strong accuracy. Instead of chasing a single universal number, compare your current results with your own goals and progress.

How Is Typing Speed Measured?

Typing speed is usually measured in words per minute, or WPM.

In standardized typing measurements, a word is commonly treated as five characters. This provides a consistent way to measure typing performance even when a passage contains words of different lengths.

A useful typing test can provide several pieces of information:

  • Words per minute
  • Typing accuracy
  • Number of errors
  • Characters typed
  • Performance over the length of the test

WPM is useful, but it should not be interpreted by itself. Two people with the same WPM can have very different levels of accuracy and control.

General Typing Speed Benchmarks

Typing ability varies considerably. The ranges below are best treated as general reference points rather than strict standards.

Typing Speed General Level What It May Indicate
Under 20 WPM Early learner You may still be learning key locations and becoming comfortable with the keyboard.
20–30 WPM Beginner You are developing keyboard familiarity, rhythm, and basic control.
30–40 WPM Developing You may be comfortable with many everyday computer tasks but still have room to improve efficiency.
40–50 WPM Practical This can be a comfortable range for many everyday school, communication, and computer tasks when accuracy is strong.
50–60 WPM Good You may be able to handle regular typing tasks efficiently without feeling rushed.
60–80 WPM Strong You likely have good keyboard familiarity and can type efficiently when accuracy remains high.
80–100 WPM Fast This represents fast typing for many everyday situations, especially when maintained over longer tests.
100+ WPM Very fast You have developed substantial speed, although accuracy and consistency still determine how useful that speed is.

These categories are not requirements. A person's ideal typing speed depends on what they need to accomplish.

What Does Your WPM Score Mean?

Instead of asking only whether a score is “good,” it can be more useful to ask what that score tells you about your current typing.

20 WPM

At around 20 WPM, focus on learning key locations, using consistent fingers, and reducing long pauses between keystrokes.

30 WPM

At around 30 WPM, you may already be comfortable with basic computer use. Improving keyboard familiarity and rhythm can help you progress.

40 WPM

Around 40 WPM can be practical for many everyday tasks. If your accuracy is strong, your next goal may be maintaining that control while gradually increasing speed.

50 WPM

At approximately 50 WPM, many routine typing activities can feel comfortable. Longer tests can show whether you can maintain this pace consistently.

60 WPM

A consistent 60 WPM with good accuracy represents solid keyboard control for many computer users.

80 WPM

At 80 WPM, accuracy and consistency become especially important. A short burst at this speed is different from maintaining it during longer work.

100+ WPM

Typing above 100 WPM is very fast for many everyday purposes. At this level, small improvements in accuracy and consistency may be more useful than simply chasing a higher number.

Your Personal Best

Your most useful benchmark may be your own previous performance. Tracking several tests can show whether your normal speed and accuracy are improving.

Why Accuracy Changes the Meaning of WPM

Suppose two people complete the same typing test.

Typist WPM Accuracy Interpretation
A 70 WPM 88% Fast, but frequent mistakes may require substantial correction.
B 55 WPM 98% Slower headline speed, but much cleaner typing.

The higher WPM result does not automatically mean better real-world performance. Correcting mistakes takes time and interrupts rhythm.

If your WPM rises while your accuracy falls sharply, you may be typing beyond your current level of control.

Useful practice rule: Try to increase speed without allowing accuracy to collapse. If errors suddenly increase, reduce your pace slightly and rebuild control.

Why Your Typing Speed Changes With Test Length

A one-minute typing test and a ten-minute typing test do not measure exactly the same thing.

1 Minute Test

A 1 minute typing test is useful for quick practice and frequent WPM checks. Because it is short, you may be able to maintain a faster pace for the entire test.

5 Minute Test

A 5 minute typing test provides more time for mistakes, difficult words, and changes in concentration to affect your result. It can provide a useful picture of consistency.

10 Minute Test

A 10 minute typing test adds an endurance component. Maintaining good posture, concentration, rhythm, and accuracy becomes more important.

For example, someone might type 65 WPM during a one-minute test but average 56 WPM during a ten-minute test. That does not necessarily mean anything is wrong. The longer test simply asks the typist to sustain performance for more time.

Should You Compare Yourself With Other Typists?

Benchmarks can provide context, but they should not become the only way you judge progress.

Different typing tests use different passages, durations, scoring methods, and difficulty levels. Your concentration, fatigue, keyboard, familiarity with the text, and number of corrections can also affect a result.

For practice purposes, comparing your current results with your own earlier results can be more useful.

For example, improving from 28 WPM at 90% accuracy to 36 WPM at 97% accuracy represents meaningful progress even if another person types much faster.

What Should Your Next Typing Goal Be?

A useful goal should be based on your current performance rather than an arbitrary number.

If You Type Under 30 WPM

Concentrate on keyboard familiarity, basic finger placement, and accuracy. Try to reduce pauses and avoid rushing.

If You Type 30–40 WPM

Work on smoother rhythm and repeated problem keys. Gradually reduce how often you need to look at the keyboard.

If You Type 40–60 WPM

Focus on maintaining strong accuracy while gradually increasing your comfortable pace. Add longer passages to your practice.

If You Type 60–80 WPM

Look for smaller weaknesses such as punctuation, numbers, difficult letter combinations, or loss of speed during longer sessions.

If You Type Above 80 WPM

Instead of chasing speed alone, measure consistency, accuracy, endurance, and performance on unfamiliar text.

Typing Speed for School and Everyday Computer Use

There is no single WPM number every student or computer user must reach.

For schoolwork, useful typing means being able to write assignments, search for information, communicate, and complete computer-based work without the keyboard becoming a major obstacle.

For everyday computer use, comfort and accuracy may matter more than achieving an unusually high WPM score.

What About Typing Speed for Jobs?

Typing requirements vary by employer and position. There is no universal WPM requirement for all office, customer service, administrative, remote, or data-entry jobs.

If a job advertisement specifies a required typing speed, use that requirement as your target rather than relying on a general internet benchmark.

For typing-heavy work, accuracy can be particularly important because incorrect names, numbers, messages, or records may need to be corrected later.

If you are preparing for employment, practice using a longer typing test so you can see whether you maintain your performance beyond a short one-minute attempt.

How to Improve Your WPM Without Sacrificing Accuracy

If you want a higher typing score, avoid simply repeating speed tests and hoping the number increases.

Use your results to decide what to practice.

  • Practice regularly instead of relying on occasional long sessions.
  • Work on accuracy before forcing maximum speed.
  • Use consistent finger placement.
  • Reduce unnecessary keyboard watching gradually.
  • Practice the letters and combinations that cause repeated errors.
  • Use complete paragraphs to develop rhythm and endurance.
  • Compare both WPM and accuracy between sessions.
  • Use longer tests occasionally to check consistency.

Track Improvement Instead of Chasing One Score

A single typing test can be affected by an unusually easy passage, a difficult passage, distractions, fatigue, or simply having a particularly good or bad attempt.

A better approach is to record multiple sessions.

For example:

Session WPM Accuracy
1 37 94%
2 39 95%
3 41 96%
4 42 97%

This pattern is more informative than a single personal-best score because both speed and accuracy are moving in a positive direction.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is 40 WPM a good typing speed?

For many everyday computer tasks, 40 WPM can be a practical typing speed when accuracy is strong. Whether it is a good result for you depends on your current skill level and what you need to accomplish.

Is 60 WPM a good typing speed?

A consistent 60 WPM with good accuracy represents comfortable typing performance for many computer users. Maintaining that speed during longer typing sessions is more demanding than reaching it briefly.

Is 80 WPM fast?

For many everyday typing situations, 80 WPM represents fast performance. Accuracy and the ability to maintain the pace over longer passages are still important.

Is 100 WPM good?

Typing 100 WPM is very fast for many everyday purposes. At that level, accuracy, consistency, and control are more useful measures than speed alone.

Why is my typing speed different every time?

Normal variation can come from passage difficulty, test duration, concentration, fatigue, unfamiliar words, punctuation, and the number of corrections you make.

Should I practice speed or accuracy first?

Accuracy is a better starting point. Once correct keystrokes become more automatic, you can gradually increase your pace without developing as many error-prone habits.

Which typing test should I use?

Use a one-minute test for quick checks, a five-minute test for consistency, and a ten-minute test when you want to measure endurance and sustained performance.
