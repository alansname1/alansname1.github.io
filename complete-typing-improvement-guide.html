Typing faster is useful, but speed by itself is not the goal. Useful typing combines speed, accuracy, consistency, and control.

If you can type quickly for 20 seconds but make frequent mistakes during a longer task, your typing still needs work. A better approach is to establish your current ability, identify what is slowing you down, practice deliberately, and measure your progress over time.

This guide gives you a practical system you can follow using free typing tests and short daily practice sessions. You do not need expensive software, a special keyboard, or hours of practice every day.

Start by Measuring Your Current Typing Speed

Before trying to improve, establish a baseline. Without a starting measurement, it is difficult to know whether your practice is actually working.

Take a typing test while sitting comfortably and typing normally. Do not deliberately slow down just to improve your accuracy, and do not rush simply to produce a higher WPM score.

Record three things:

  • Words per minute (WPM)
  • Accuracy
  • Errors or recurring mistakes

For a more useful starting point, take the 1 minute typing test three times. Rest briefly between attempts and record all three results.

Test WPM Accuracy Observation
1 38 94% Several corrections
2 41 96% Smoother rhythm
3 39 95% Looked at keyboard twice

In this example, a reasonable starting point would be approximately 39–40 WPM with about 95% accuracy.

Don't use only your highest score. Your goal is to understand your normal performance, not find the single best result you can produce.

What Does WPM Actually Mean?

Typing speed is usually measured in words per minute, commonly abbreviated as WPM.

In standardized typing measurements, a “word” is commonly treated as five characters. That makes WPM a standardized way to compare typing performance rather than simply counting the number of dictionary words entered.

Your typing test may also report accuracy. That measurement matters because a high WPM result combined with frequent mistakes does not necessarily represent efficient typing.

For everyday computer use, concentrate on two measurements:

  • Speed: how quickly you enter text.
  • Accuracy: how closely your typing matches the intended text.

You can learn more about typical ranges in our guide to good typing speeds and typing speed chart.

Accuracy Should Come Before Maximum Speed

One of the easiest ways to develop poor typing habits is to push for maximum speed before you have good control.

Imagine two people completing the same typing test:

  • Person A types 65 WPM but makes frequent mistakes.
  • Person B types 52 WPM with very few mistakes.

Person A has the higher headline speed, but Person B may be more efficient once corrections and interruptions are considered.

If your accuracy drops noticeably whenever you try to type faster, reduce your pace slightly and concentrate on clean keystrokes.

A simple rule: Clean first. Fast second.

Once accurate movements become more familiar, gradually increase your pace.

Learn Consistent Finger Placement

You do not have to become a perfect touch typist overnight. However, using consistent finger positions can reduce unnecessary hand movement.

Left Hand Home Row

  • Pinky: A
  • Ring finger: S
  • Middle finger: D
  • Index finger: F

Right Hand Home Row

  • Index finger: J
  • Middle finger: K
  • Ring finger: L
  • Pinky: ;

The F and J keys normally have small raised marks. You can use these physical guides to locate the home-row position without looking down.

After reaching for another key, allow your fingers to return naturally toward their starting positions.

If you currently type with only two or three fingers, do not try to change every habit at once. Start slowly and become familiar with which fingers are responsible for different areas of the keyboard.

Use the keyboard finger placement guide for more focused practice.

Stop Looking at the Keyboard Gradually

Looking at the keyboard is common among beginners. Trying to stop completely on the first day can make practice frustrating.

Instead, reduce the habit gradually.

Try This Exercise

  1. Place your fingers on the home row.
  2. Type one short sentence while looking at the screen.
  3. If you lose your position, use the raised marks on F and J to relocate your index fingers.
  4. Look down only when necessary.
  5. Repeat the sentence.

The goal is not to prove that you can type without looking. The goal is to make looking at the keyboard less necessary over time.

Diagnose What Is Actually Slowing You Down

“Type faster” is too vague to be a useful practice goal.

After a typing test, ask yourself what actually caused your mistakes or pauses.

Repeated Letter Errors

If you repeatedly mistype the same letters, practice words containing those letters rather than taking endless speed tests.

Poor Rhythm

If you type in short bursts followed by long pauses, practice complete sentences at a comfortable pace. Aim for smooth movement instead of maximum speed.

Looking Down Frequently

If your eyes constantly move between the keyboard and screen, work specifically on keyboard familiarity.

Backspace Dependence

If you repeatedly type the wrong key and immediately hit Backspace, reduce your pace. Constant correction can interrupt your rhythm.

Difficulty With Capitals and Punctuation

Normal writing contains commas, periods, apostrophes, capital letters, numbers, and symbols. Practice realistic text instead of only easy lowercase words.

Knowing your specific weakness lets you choose a useful exercise rather than simply repeating tests.

A Practical 15-Minute Daily Typing Routine

You do not need to spend hours practicing. A focused 15-minute session can give you a clear purpose without becoming overwhelming.

Minutes 1–3: Warm Up

Type slowly and comfortably while concentrating on:

  • Correct finger movement
  • Relaxed hands
  • Comfortable posture
  • Smooth rhythm

Do not worry about your WPM during the warm-up.

Minutes 4–8: Accuracy Practice

Type a paragraph at a controlled pace.

When you make an error, notice what happened. Did you use the wrong finger? Did you rush? Did you reverse two letters?

The goal is to recognize patterns rather than merely finish the passage.

Try our typing practice paragraphs for this part of your routine.

Minutes 9–12: Target One Weakness

Choose one problem from a recent test.

  • Trouble with R and T? Practice words containing those letters.
  • Number errors? Practice number sequences.
  • Punctuation errors? Type sentences containing commas and apostrophes.
  • Frequent keyboard watching? Practice short sentences while keeping your eyes on the screen.

Minutes 13–15: Test Yourself

Finish with a short timed test.

Record your WPM and accuracy, but do not worry if today's result is slightly lower than yesterday's.

Typing performance varies between tests. Look for trends over multiple sessions.

A Four-Week Typing Improvement Plan

Instead of trying to increase your WPM every day, give each week a specific purpose.

Week 1: Establish Control

Concentrate on:

  • Home-row awareness
  • Correct keystrokes
  • Relaxed hands
  • Accuracy
  • Identifying recurring errors

Take a short baseline test at the beginning of the week and record the result.

Week 2: Build Keyboard Familiarity

Continue accuracy work while reducing how often you need to look at the keyboard.

Add:

  • Common word drills
  • Short sentences
  • Capital letters
  • Basic punctuation

Week 3: Improve Rhythm

Start working on smoother, more continuous typing.

Practice complete paragraphs rather than isolated letters. Pay attention to unnecessary pauses and repeated corrections.

Week 4: Measure Progress

Repeat the same testing method you used at the beginning.

If you originally took three 1-minute tests, take three more under similar conditions.

Compare:

  • WPM
  • Accuracy
  • Common errors
  • Consistency between tests
  • How often you need to look at the keyboard
Remember: A higher WPM is not the only sign of progress. Fewer mistakes, smoother typing, and more consistent results also matter.

Practice Realistic Text

Random letters can help with specific keyboard movements, but most people use typing for sentences, messages, assignments, forms, reports, and other real text.

Include practice passages containing:

  • Uppercase and lowercase letters
  • Common punctuation
  • Short and long words
  • Numbers
  • Paragraph breaks

This makes practice more representative of everyday computer use.

Keep Your Hands Relaxed

Typing faster does not require hitting the keys harder.

Excessive tension can make your movements less comfortable and less efficient.

While practicing:

  • Keep your shoulders relaxed.
  • Avoid pressing keys harder than necessary.
  • Keep your wrists in a comfortable position.
  • Sit at a reasonable distance from the keyboard.
  • Take breaks if your hands become tired or uncomfortable.

Typing practice should not hurt. Stop if you experience pain or persistent discomfort rather than trying to type through it.

How to Track Your Typing Progress

Do not judge improvement from a single typing test.

Create a simple record containing your date, test length, WPM, accuracy, and a short note about the session.

Date Test Length WPM Accuracy Notes
July 1 1 minute 38 94% Looked at keyboard often
July 8 1 minute 40 96% Fewer corrections
July 15 1 minute 42 96% Better rhythm

The notes can be just as useful as the numbers.

You may discover that your WPM has changed only slightly while your accuracy has improved significantly. You may also discover that your one-minute score is strong but your five-minute result drops noticeably.

Those observations tell you what to practice next.

When Should You Try to Type Faster?

Increase your speed gradually when your current pace feels controlled.

  1. Type at a comfortable speed.
  2. Maintain good accuracy.
  3. Repeat until the movement feels natural.
  4. Increase your pace slightly.
  5. Reduce your speed again if your errors increase sharply.

You do not need to chase a personal record during every practice session.

Your goal is to make a faster pace feel comfortable and repeatable rather than forced.

Common Typing Practice Mistakes

Testing Instead of Practicing

Taking ten speed tests in a row measures your typing repeatedly, but it does not necessarily address the weakness holding you back.

Spend more time practicing specific skills than repeatedly measuring them.

Comparing Yourself With Other Typists

Someone else's WPM tells you little about your own improvement.

Compare your current results with your previous results.

Ignoring Accuracy

A higher WPM score can feel rewarding, but sacrificing control simply to increase the number can reinforce poor habits.

Practicing Only Easy Text

If you always avoid punctuation, capitals, numbers, or difficult words, those areas are unlikely to improve.

Changing Technique Constantly

Give yourself enough time to become comfortable with a technique before deciding it is not working.

A Simple Practice Session You Can Try Today

Step 1: Establish Your Baseline

Take three 1 minute typing tests and write down your WPM and accuracy for each.

Step 2: Identify One Weakness

Look for one recurring problem. Perhaps you frequently look at the keyboard, reverse letters, make punctuation errors, or lose rhythm.

Step 3: Practice That Weakness

Spend approximately ten focused minutes working on the specific issue rather than taking more speed tests.

Step 4: Take One Final Test

Take another short typing test. Do not worry about whether the score immediately increased. Pay attention to whether your typing felt more controlled.

Step 5: Record the Result

Add the result to your progress dashboard and compare your performance across several sessions.

This approach gives each practice session a purpose instead of simply repeating tests and hoping your WPM increases.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a good typing speed?

There is no single WPM score everyone needs. The appropriate speed depends on how you use a keyboard. For many everyday tasks, comfortable and accurate typing matters as much as reaching a particular number. See our good typing speed guide for more context.

Should I practice typing speed or accuracy first?

Start with accuracy and control. Once correct keystrokes become more automatic, gradually increase your speed while trying to maintain that accuracy.

How often should I practice typing?

Regular short sessions are often easier to sustain than occasional marathon sessions. Focused practice on most days gives you repeated opportunities to reinforce better habits.

Why is my typing speed different every time?

Typing results can vary because of passage difficulty, test length, concentration, fatigue, familiarity with the words, and normal variation in performance. Look at several results rather than judging yourself from one test.

Is touch typing worth learning?

Touch typing can be useful because it reduces dependence on looking at the keyboard and encourages more consistent finger movement. You do not have to master it immediately.

Why am I not getting faster at typing?

You may be testing yourself repeatedly without practicing the specific weakness limiting your speed. Check for recurring errors, keyboard watching, poor rhythm, difficult key combinations, or accuracy problems, then practice that specific area.

Final Thoughts

Improving your typing is not about forcing your fingers to move as quickly as possible.

A better process is:

  1. Establish your baseline.
  2. Identify your weaknesses.
  3. Practice accuracy and consistent finger movement.
  4. Work specifically on recurring errors.
  5. Use different test lengths.
  6. Record your results.
  7. Judge progress over multiple sessions rather than one test.

Start with your current ability instead of worrying about someone else's WPM.

Take a short typing test, record your speed and accuracy, choose one weakness to work on, and use your next practice session to improve that specific skill.

That turns typing practice from simply chasing a number into a measurable process for building better keyboard skills.
