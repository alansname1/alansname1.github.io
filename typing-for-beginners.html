Where Should a Beginner Start?

Learning to type can feel difficult at first because you are trying to remember key locations, move several fingers, read the screen, and avoid mistakes at the same time.

The easiest approach is to break typing into smaller skills.

A beginner can start by learning:

  • Where important keys are located
  • How to place both hands comfortably
  • How to use more than one or two fingers
  • How to type simple words accurately
  • How to use spaces and punctuation
  • How to reduce keyboard watching gradually
  • How to practice without rushing
Beginner principle: your first goal is not to be fast. Your first goal is to make the keyboard feel less unfamiliar.

What Is Touch Typing?

Touch typing is a method of typing where you rely increasingly on finger position and learned movement instead of searching visually for every key.

As keyboard familiarity develops, you may be able to keep your eyes on the screen for longer periods while your fingers find commonly used keys.

Touch typing can help with:

Keyboard Familiarity

Repeated movements can make key locations easier to remember.

More Consistent Movement

Using assigned fingers can reduce random hand movement across the keyboard.

Less Keyboard Watching

Over time, you can rely less on visually searching for every letter.

Better Rhythm

Familiar movements can make typing feel smoother and less interrupted.

For a deeper explanation, read Learn Touch Typing.

Home Row Finger Placement

The traditional starting position for touch typing is called the home row.

Hand Finger Home Key
Left Pinky A
Left Ring finger S
Left Middle finger D
Left Index finger F
Right Index finger J
Right Middle finger K
Right Ring finger L
Right Pinky ;

The F and J keys usually contain small raised marks. These can help you find the home position without looking directly at the keyboard.

Your thumbs can rest near the Spacebar.

See Keyboard Finger Placement for more detailed practice.

Do You Need Perfect Finger Placement Immediately?

No.

Beginners often need time to become comfortable using all their fingers.

Instead of trying to remember the entire keyboard in one session, build the habit gradually:

  1. Learn the home-row position.
  2. Practice a few letters at a time.
  3. Return your fingers to the home row after reaching for another key.
  4. Use the F and J markers when you lose your position.
  5. Gradually add more keys.

Consistency matters more than trying to become technically perfect on the first day.

Your First Beginner Exercise

Start by practicing easy home-row combinations.

Home Row Practice

Type slowly. Try to return each finger to its starting position after every movement.

Move From Letters to Words

Once individual keys feel more familiar, practice complete words.

Simple Word Practice

and the this that with from time make work people simple typing practice better keyboard

Do not worry about how quickly you finish the line.

Instead, notice:

  • Which letters require you to look down
  • Which words cause repeated mistakes
  • Whether one hand feels more comfortable than the other
  • Whether your hands remain relaxed

Then Practice Complete Sentences

Beginner Sentence Practice

Typing becomes easier when I practice slowly and accurately. I will focus on the correct keys, keep my hands relaxed, and build speed gradually.

Complete sentences teach more than letters. They also help you practice:

  • Spacing
  • Capital letters
  • Periods
  • Word transitions
  • Reading slightly ahead

Accuracy Before Maximum Speed

Beginners sometimes assume that typing improvement means moving their fingers as fast as possible.

That often creates unnecessary mistakes.

A more useful process is:

  1. Type at a comfortable pace.
  2. Reduce repeated mistakes.
  3. Make movements more familiar.
  4. Increase pace slightly.
  5. Check whether accuracy remains stable.

Use Typing Accuracy Practice when repeated mistakes are slowing your progress.

Is There a Required Beginner WPM?

There is no single WPM score every beginner needs to reach.

Beginners differ in:

  • Age
  • Keyboard experience
  • How often they use computers
  • Whether they already use several fingers
  • Whether they are learning touch typing for the first time
  • How accurately they type

Instead of judging yourself by one number, look at stages of development.

Stage Useful Goal
Learning keys Find common letters more easily.
Building control Reduce repeated mistakes.
Typing sentences Use spacing and punctuation accurately.
Short test practice Develop steady rhythm.
Longer typing Maintain accuracy and comfort for several minutes.

When you want to understand WPM benchmarks more broadly, read What Is a Good Typing Speed?.

Is Looking at the Keyboard Bad?

Looking at the keyboard is normal for beginners.

The problem is not that you look down occasionally. The goal is simply to depend on it less as your memory improves.

Try this:

  1. Place your fingers on the home row.
  2. Look at the screen.
  3. Type an easy word.
  4. If you lose your position, feel for the F and J markers.
  5. Look down briefly only when necessary.
  6. Return your eyes to the screen.

Reducing keyboard watching gradually is usually more manageable than trying to stop instantly.

Common Beginner Typing Mistakes

Mistake Better Approach

2 minutes Sentence typing

A Four-Week Beginner Learning Plan

Week 1: Keyboard Familiarity

  • Learn the home row.
  • Practice common letters.
  • Use short word drills.
  • Focus heavily on accuracy.

Week 2: Words and Sentences

  • Practice common words.
  • Add capitalization.
  • Practice spaces and periods.
  • Begin reducing keyboard watching.

Week 3: Paragraphs and Short Tests

  • Practice short paragraphs.
  • Take occasional 1-minute tests.
  • Record repeated mistakes.
  • Practice difficult words separately.

Week 4: Consistency

  • Compare several test results.
  • Practice longer passages.
  • Try a 5-minute test when ready.
  • Continue working on accuracy.

This schedule is only a framework. Move faster or slower depending on what feels comfortable.

How to Track Beginner Progress

Typing progress is more than a personal-best WPM.

Track:

  • WPM
  • Accuracy
  • Common errors
  • How often you look at the keyboard
  • How comfortable your finger movements feel
  • Whether longer passages feel easier

Signs That You Are Improving

You may be improving even when your WPM changes only slightly.

Positive signs include:

  • You find keys with less hesitation.
  • You make fewer repeated mistakes.
  • You look down less often.
  • Your typing feels smoother.
  • You use more fingers naturally.
  • You can type longer without losing concentration.
  • Your accuracy stays stronger as speed increases.

What Should You Learn After the Basics?

Once simple typing feels comfortable, continue with:

  1. Touch typing practice
  2. Better finger placement
  3. Accuracy drills
  4. Focused typing exercises
  5. Paragraph practice
  6. Longer timed tests

Frequently Asked Questions

What should a beginner learn first?

Start with keyboard familiarity, basic finger placement, accurate keystrokes, simple words, and short sentences.

Should beginners learn touch typing?

Touch typing can help develop more consistent finger movements and reduce reliance on looking at the keyboard.

Should beginners focus on speed or accuracy?

Accuracy and controlled movements provide a useful foundation. Increase speed gradually as your keyboard familiarity improves.

Is it normal to look at the keyboard?

Yes. Looking at the keyboard is normal while learning. The goal is to reduce it gradually as key locations become more familiar.

How often should beginners practice?

Regular short sessions are usually easier to maintain than occasional long practice. Consistency matters more than spending a very long time in one session.

How do I know whether I am improving?

Look at WPM, accuracy, repeated errors, keyboard watching, comfort, and consistency across several practice sessions.
